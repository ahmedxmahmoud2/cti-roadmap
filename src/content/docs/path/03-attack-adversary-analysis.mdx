The goal is not to memorise ATT&CK techniques. It is to use ATT&CK to analyse how an adversary behaves.

## Study from

<Course title="Using ATT&CK for Cyber Threat Intelligence" url="https://attack.mitre.org/resources/learn-more-about-attack/training/cti/" by="MITRE" cost="free" time="~4 hours">
Five modules of videos and exercises from the ATT&CK team: mapping finished reports and raw data to ATT&CK, then comparing groups to find defensive priorities.
</Course>

<Course title="ATT&CK Groups" url="https://attack.mitre.org/groups/" by="MITRE" cost="free">
Your reference for every group, its techniques and the reports behind them.
</Course>

**Tool:** [ATT&CK Navigator](https://mitre-attack.github.io/attack-navigator/) to build and compare technique layers.

## What you will learn

### The ATT&CK framework

Tactics, techniques, sub-techniques, groups, software, campaigns, mitigations and detection strategies. Understand the chain: threat actor → campaign → malware → technique → detection.

### Adversary TTP analysis

Analyse each stage of an attack: initial access, execution, persistence, privilege escalation, defense evasion, credential access, discovery, lateral movement, collection, command and control, exfiltration and impact.

### Campaign analysis

Link several incidents or reports into one campaign using infrastructure overlap, malware and code similarities, TTP similarities, targeting patterns, operational timing and campaign artifacts.

One shared technique is not enough to prove the same group is behind two attacks.

## Project: campaign analysis

Analyse a real APT campaign and produce:

- A campaign timeline.
- Targeted victims.
- The attack chain.
- Malware used.
- Infrastructure.
- An ATT&CK mapping with a Navigator layer.
- Detection opportunities.
- Intelligence gaps.
- An attribution assessment.

## You are ready to move on when

- You can map a report to ATT&CK without guessing.
- You can explain why two incidents do or do not belong to the same campaign.
